## Chapter 2: Choosing Passwords That Are Easy to Guess

### 1. The Everyday Habit

Karan supports Chennai Super Kings and wears jersey number 7. His main password is `Dhoni@7777`. It has a capital, a symbol, and numbers, so the sign-up page called it "strong." *(Fictional.)*

### 2. Why People Make This Mistake

We pick what we can remember, and what we remember is personal: names, birthdays, teams, phone numbers. Sign-up forms also teach us that a capital letter plus a symbol equals safe, so we make tiny changes to satisfy the form and move on.

### 3. What Can Go Wrong?

A password built from personal details or common patterns can be guessed far faster than people expect. Much of what makes a password guessable is on our public profiles: pet names, favourite players, college, birth year.

If someone guesses it, they can enter your account as you.

### 4. How an Attacker Might Exploit It

Attackers do not type guesses by hand. Software tries lists of the most common passwords first, then variations such as capital first letters, a number on the end, or "@" for "a." When they know a little about you, they add your name or team. Trying many guesses automatically is called **brute-forcing**, though in practice the cleverest guessing is about *likely* passwords, not every possible one.

Well-run services slow this down by locking accounts or blocking repeated attempts, but you shouldn't depend on that.

### 5. A Realistic Example

**HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE:** A classmate who knows Karan's team and jersey number tries a handful of obvious combinations on his email login. Without any technical skill, the third guess works.

### 6. Warning Signs to Watch For

- Your password contains your name, birth year, team, or phone number
- It is short (NIST's current guidance sets 15 characters as the minimum for password-only logins [3])
- You change only a digit or symbol each time
- It appears in the "weak" or "reused" list of your password manager

> **MYTH vs. REALITY**
> *Myth:* "It has a capital letter and a symbol, so it's strong."
> *Reality:* Predictable patterns are easy to try. NIST's current guidance for service providers moves away from forcing such rules and towards length and blocking commonly used or compromised passwords [3].

### 7. How to Protect Yourself

**Right now:** Replace any password that includes personal details, starting with your email and money apps.

**Over time:**
- Use a **passphrase**: several unrelated words, such as four or more, long enough to be hard to guess. Let a password manager or a generator choose them; words you think of yourself tend to be predictable.
- Let your password manager create and fill long random passwords for everything else, so you only memorise one passphrase.
- For phone lock codes, avoid birthdays and repeating digits. Use a longer code if your phone allows it.
- Don't rely on frequent forced changes. Change a password when it may have been exposed [3].

> **SECURITY TIP:** Length helps more than cleverness. A longer passphrase generally beats a short "complex" one.

### 8. If You've Already Made This Mistake

1. Change the weak password now, starting with email, banking/UPI, and shopping.
2. If you used it elsewhere, treat those accounts as exposed too (see Chapter 1).
3. Turn on multi-factor authentication (see Chapter 4).
4. Check recent account activity and signed-in devices.
5. If money moved without your approval, contact your bank and report through official channels (Chapter 1).

### 9. The 30-Second Security Fix

Pick your most important account and change its password to a passphrase of four or more unrelated words, generated by your password manager.

### 10. Remember This

**If a friend who knows you could guess it, so can a stranger with patience.**
