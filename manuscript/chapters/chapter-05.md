## Chapter 5: Sharing Passwords or Storing Them Insecurely

### 1. The Everyday Habit

Divya keeps her passwords in a phone note called "Passwords." Her sister has the streaming login, sent over WhatsApp. A sticky note on her laptop lid holds the Wi-Fi password. *(Fictional.)*

### 2. Why People Make This Mistake

Convenience. Passwords need to be somewhere, sharing a subscription saves money, and "it's just family" feels harmless.

### 3. What Can Go Wrong?

Passwords stored in plain notes, spreadsheets, screenshots, or chat history are readable by anyone who gets into that app, backup, or lost phone. Messages are forwarded, synced to other devices, and backed up. Shared passwords also mean you can't tell who signed in, and you can't remove one person without changing it for everyone.

### 4. How an Attacker Might Exploit It

An attacker who gets into one place, for example your cloud notes, email, or a lost unlocked phone, may find every password at once. Files named "passwords" are an obvious thing to look for, so storing them there offers no cover. Shoulder-surfing, meaning someone simply reading a visible screen or note, needs no technology at all.

### 5. A Realistic Example

**HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE:** Divya's phone is stolen while unlocked at a busy railway station. The thief opens her notes app, finds "Passwords," and reaches her email within seconds.

### 6. Warning Signs to Watch For

- Passwords visible in notes, photos, spreadsheets, or old chats
- Passwords sent as plain text messages
- Accounts that "everyone" shares
- A shared account showing sign-ins from unfamiliar places

### 7. How to Protect Yourself

**Right now:** Delete screenshots and chat messages containing passwords, then change those passwords.

**Over time:**
- Use a **password manager**, an app that stores passwords in encrypted form behind one master passphrase. CISA recommends them [4]. Many phones and browsers include a free one; check your platform's help pages.
- Protect the manager with a long, unique passphrase and turn on multi-factor authentication for it (Chapter 4).
- For shared subscriptions, use the service's own **profiles or family-sharing** features instead of sharing one login.
- If you must share a password, do it in person or through your manager's sharing feature, never as a plain message, and change it afterwards.
- A written note kept in a secure place at home is better than a note on your phone, but not carried with you.

> **SECURITY TIP:** A password manager does create a single point of failure, which is why the master passphrase and MFA matter. For most people, that trade-off is better than reuse or loose notes.

### 8. If You've Already Made This Mistake

1. Remove passwords from notes, screenshots, and chats, including backups where you can.
2. Change every password that was stored or shared, starting with email and money apps.
3. Sign out of all devices on shared accounts.
4. Move to a password manager and turn on MFA.
5. If someone accessed an account without permission and money moved, contact your bank and report via **1930** and **cybercrime.gov.in** [5].

### 9. The 30-Second Security Fix

Open your notes app, search for "password," and delete or move anything you find.

### 10. Remember This

**A password in a plain note is like a house key under the doormat, and everyone knows where to look.**
