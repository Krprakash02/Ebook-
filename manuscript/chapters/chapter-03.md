## Chapter 3: Ignoring Data Breach Notices and Never Checking Whether You Were Exposed

### 1. The Everyday Habit

Meera gets an email: "We recently discovered unauthorised access to our systems. Your data may be affected." It is from an app she barely remembers using. She swipes it away. *(Fictional.)*

### 2. Why People Make This Mistake

Breach emails are dull, and they arrive often enough to feel like noise. Many people assume "I don't use that site anymore, so it doesn't matter" or "the company will fix it."

### 3. What Can Go Wrong?

Your old accounts can still hold your email, password, phone number, or address. If that information leaks, it can be used to try the same password elsewhere (Chapter 1) or to make scam messages that sound convincing because they use your real details.

A leak does not mean you will be hacked, but it changes what you should do next.

### 4. How an Attacker Might Exploit It

Leaked data can be shared or sold. Criminals may use the email-and-password pairs for automated login attempts, and the phone numbers and names for targeted phishing, where the message knows enough about you to seem genuine. The longer you wait, the more time they have.

### 5. A Realistic Example

**HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE:** Months after Meera ignores the notice, she gets a call from "the app's support team" who correctly quote her name and phone number and ask for an OTP "to secure your account." The details were real, taken from the leak. The request was a scam.

### 6. Warning Signs to Watch For

- A notice from a service you have used
- Sudden spikes of spam, or scam calls that know your name
- Reset emails you didn't request

> **RED FLAG:** Scammers also send *fake* breach notices. Don't click a link inside one. Open the official website or app yourself.

### 7. How to Protect Yourself

**Right now:** Check your main email address on **Have I Been Pwned** (haveibeenpwned.com), a free service created by security researcher Troy Hunt that reports which known breaches included your address [7]. Type the address in yourself.

**Over time:**
- Use your password manager's built-in checker for reused, weak, or exposed passwords. Google's Password Manager, for example, offers a "Password Checkup" [11]; consult your provider's help pages for current steps.
- Subscribe to breach alerts for your main addresses, if the service offers them.
- Delete accounts you no longer use (Chapter 37).

### 8. If You've Already Made This Mistake

1. Change the password on the affected service. If you reused it, change it everywhere (Chapter 1).
2. Look at *what* leaked. Passwords: act now. Phone number or address: expect more scam calls and messages. Financial details: tell your bank.
3. Turn on multi-factor authentication.
4. Treat unexpected calls or messages that quote your personal details with extra suspicion. Having your details does not make a caller genuine.
5. If you lose money, contact your bank immediately and report through official channels (**1930**, **cybercrime.gov.in**) [5]. Confirm current procedures on the official sites.

### 9. The 30-Second Security Fix

Go to haveibeenpwned.com, type your main email address, and read the result.

### 10. Remember This

**A breach notice isn't junk mail. It's a smoke alarm. Check it, then act.**
