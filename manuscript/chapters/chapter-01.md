## Chapter 1: Using the Same Password Everywhere

### 1. The Everyday Habit

Ananya, a second-year engineering student, uses `Ananya@2004` for her college portal, Instagram, streaming account, and the shopping app where her card is saved. It is easy to remember, and nothing has ever gone wrong. *(Fictional.)*

### 2. Why People Make This Mistake

Dozens of accounts, one memory. Add the quiet belief that "nobody would bother hacking me." Attackers rarely pick individuals. They work in bulk.

### 3. What Can Go Wrong?

Websites get breached, and you cannot control how well a small forum protects its data. Reuse turns one site's failure into failure everywhere: your strongest account is only as safe as the weakest site sharing its password.

The results can include account takeover, fraudulent purchases, messages sent in your name, and, if your email falls, password resets across your other accounts.

### 4. How an Attacker Might Exploit It

Criminals take leaked email-and-password pairs and use automated software to try them on other popular services. This is called **credential stuffing**. It costs almost nothing and only needs *some* people to have reused a password. You can be caught without anyone ever targeting you personally.

### 5. A Realistic Example

**DOCUMENTED INCIDENT [1][2]:** In October 2023, genetic-testing company 23andMe disclosed unauthorised access to customer accounts. Reports said attackers appeared to have used credentials exposed in other breaches, and that profile data of millions of customers was exposed through a relatives-matching feature. How much blame lay with customers versus the company was publicly disputed, and details changed as the story developed.

The lesson is not "blame users." It is that one reused password can be the only door an attacker needs.

**HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE:** A cricket-fan forum Ananya joined in 2021 is breached. Months later, her leaked password is tried on a streaming service, then a shopping app, and works on both.

### 6. Warning Signs to Watch For

- Login alerts or reset emails you did not request
- Unexpected logouts
- Unfamiliar orders, subscriptions, or signed-in devices
- Friends receiving odd messages "from you"

> **RED FLAG:** There may be no sign at all. Fix reuse before you see one.

### 7. How to Protect Yourself

**Right now:** Give your **email** a unique password first, because email can reset almost everything else. Then fix banking, UPI, and shopping accounts.

**Over time:**
- Use a **password manager**, a tool that creates and stores a different strong password for each account. Many phones and browsers include a free one; a paid product is not required.
- Prefer long passwords. NIST's current guidance favours length, discourages forced composition rules, and recommends checking against known-compromised lists [3].
- Turn on **multi-factor authentication** (a second proof of identity) for email, money, shopping, and social accounts [4]. See Chapter 4.

### 8. If You've Already Made This Mistake

Most of us have. In order:

1. Change your email password to a new, unique one.
2. Change the reused password everywhere it appears, starting with banking/UPI.
3. Turn on multi-factor authentication.
4. Review recent activity and signed-in devices; sign out of any you don't recognise.
5. If money moved without your approval, tell your bank at once and report through official channels: the helpline **1930** and **cybercrime.gov.in** [5]. Bank liability for unauthorised transactions depends partly on how fast you report [6]. Confirm current procedures on the official sites.

### 9. The 30-Second Security Fix

Open your email account's security settings and change its password to one you have never used anywhere else.

### 10. Remember This

**One password, one door. Reuse it, and you've handed out copies of the key.**
